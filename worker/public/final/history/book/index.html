The parish history book

All Saints' kept an illustrated history of the parish: its buildings, its sacred objects, and the people who shaped them. Here it is, with the original book to read in full.

Our History  /  The Parish History Book

Some years ago the parish gathered its story into a single book: pencil drawings of the buildings, the histories behind the chandeliers and the font and the organ, and a careful guide to the symbolism of the windows. The pages that follow draw from that book. Everything here is taken straight from it.

Buildings and Beginnings

How the parish took shape

From a group meeting in a parlor, to two congregations ten miles apart, to one parish in a new church.

The Founding Years
Early years

A parlor in the Gibson home

For many years a group of Episcopalians met in the Concord home of Mr. and Mrs. James C. Gibson, having services in the parlor and Sunday School in the dining room.

1886

A first building, bought used

This Presbyterian church building was purchased for $500.00 in 1886 and was used for many years as the Episcopal Church in Cabarrus County.

1891

A cornerstone, and ivy from Westminster

The cornerstone of All Saints' was dated November, 1891. The church stood at the corner of Cabarrus Avenue and Spring Street. The ivy covering part of its walls was brought from Westminster Abbey by Sister Mary Joseph, who was the sister of Emily Gibson and Julia Magruder.

1892

The Rev. J. C. Davis arrives

The Reverend J. C. Davis of Athens, Georgia, became the Rector of All Saints' in December, 1892. He was the priest for over 17 years.

Kannapolis and St. James
1940s

A mission begins in Kannapolis

In the early 1940's, twenty-four interested Episcopalians gathered in an empty house at 303 Sloop Avenue to begin forming a mission church in Kannapolis. Mr. and Mrs. G.G. Allen offered the house and the property for the church.

1954

St. James, on Cook Street

Funds for the building on Cook Street were collected in 1947. Under the Reverend Martin Caldwell, Vicar from 1952–1954, "a dream that began a decade before became a reality" for St. James Church in Kannapolis. The first service was held on April 13, 1954. St. James stood from 1953 to 1968.

One Parish
1969

Two churches become one

On January 1, 1969, All Saints' and St. James held congregational meetings, with both churches approving the merger to form a stronger church to witness to Christ. Plans were drawn up for the current church building.

1972

A new church on Lake Concord Road

A four year program establishing "The Episcopal Church in Cabarrus County" climaxed on October 22, 1972 as Bishop Thomas A. Fraser dedicated the ultra-modern $270,000 church which completed the merger of All Saints and St. James Parish.

Treasures of the Parish

Objects with long stories

Many things in the church arrived by remarkable routes, and most carry someone's name.

The Bonaparte Chandeliers

Given 1937

Four chandeliers that once hung in the Paris palace of Prince deRohan Bonaparte, a cousin of Napoleon Bonaparte. After the First World War the palace was declared unsafe and dismantled, and its furnishings went to auction; Mr. Feldman, a New Orleans antique dealer, bought the sixteen smaller chandeliers and the very large one. Four came to All Saints', the gift of Mrs. Ada Rogers Gorman in tribute to her mother, Mrs. Martha Rogers, in 1937. Each holds twenty lights amid a tracery of gold leaf, and they hang in the nave today.

The Reredos

Easter 1980

The carved screen behind the altar, dedicated to Samuel I. Parker, "this most decorated soldier," as a tribute from Sam Stroup at Easter 1980. Mrs. Jane Liles chaired the committee; Sam Stroup designed it and Southern Desk Company built it, charged to "match the furniture but keep the design simple." Its seven panels echo the Gothic arch of early cathedrals, and their three portions of circles are a symbol of the Trinity.

The Organ

Schlicker Organ Company

Built by the Schlicker Organ Company of Buffalo, New York. Mr. Herman Schlicker worked out the specification with Mr. Henry Bridges of the Community School of the Arts of Charlotte and the Organ Committee, chaired by Dr. David Lockhart. It has 656 pipes, each made by hand. The 21 note set of Maryland Chimes was given in memory of Miss Isabelle Whitefield, Mrs. Mildred Mitchell Goodman, and Mrs. Mary Fisher Isenhour.

The Victorian Angel

In service since 1908

A baptismal font, a lovely Victorian angel sculpted from Carrara marble in Italy and holding a bowl at its waist. It has served as the Baptismal Font for All Saints' Church since 1908. It was a gift from Mrs. Ada Rogers Gorman.

The Columbarium

Dedicated January 1986

A structure of recessed niches to hold cremation urns, designed by William F. Freeman, an architect from High Point. Dr. David Lockhart designed the wrought iron gates marking the entrance to the garden. It was completed on December 22, 1985 and dedicated in memory of Susan Frances Lockhart by the Rt. Reverend Robert W. Estill in January, 1986.

The Parish Banner

Dedicated All Saints' Day, 1985

Designed in memory of Fenner Hammond Springs McCoy, a textile consultant in Concord for most of his life, and Myrtle Louise McCoy, a well known local artist. The banner was made in England by J. Wippell and Company of Exeter, designed with Mr. McCoy's textile background in mind and the artistic flair of Mrs. McCoy. It features a flaming sword and stylized crown, both symbols of saints and martyrs.

The Triptych Window

In the Children's Chapel

A three-panel window from the old church on Cabarrus Avenue, moved to All Saints' in 1972 and now located in the Children's Chapel. It was given in fond memory of the Reverend John Thomas Wheat, D.D., Rector 1877–1881, with the Sunday School children and other members of the church helping to fund the memorial.

The Saint James Brass

A brief history by Sam Stroup

The altar and brass from St. James Church in Kannapolis, whose history Sam Stroup recorded in detail: the altar cross, the processional cross, the candlesticks, the vesper candlesticks, and the missal stand, each given in someone's memory. The altar and brass are located in the Children's Chapel at All Saints' today.

A Guide for the Curious

Symbolism in our worship space

If you have ever wondered what the windows and crosses mean, here is the parish's own guide, compiled by a former rector, the Rev. W. Parker Marks. It is detailed, more than most will read at one sitting, and that is the point: open whichever part draws you. Every word is from the book.

The All Saints Altar Cross

The Cross Bontonee or Roman Budded Cross. The lettered points below are explained in turn.

  • A. IHS, the first three letters in the Greek name "Jesus." On the back side of the Cross at this location is the Greek CHI-RHO, the first letter in the Greek word, "Christ."
  • B. Fleur De Lis, symbolic of the Trinity.
  • C. St. Matthew, symbolized by a winged creature with a man's face because Matthew's Gospel account traces Jesus' human genealogy.
  • D. St. John, symbolized by an eagle because John's Gospel account rises to loftiest heights when speaking of Jesus and His work.
  • E. St. Mark, symbolized by a winged creature with the head of a lion. Mark's Gospel account begins with "The voice of one crying in the wilderness," suggestive of the roar of a lion.
  • F. Luke, symbolized by a winged creature with the head of an ox, the animal of sacrifice, since Luke's Gospel account stresses the atoning sacrifice of Jesus.

Matthew, John, Mark, and Luke are the Four Evangelists (evangel = Greek, "good news"), as they bring the Good News of Jesus Christ.

  • G. Sun and Moon, symbolizes that Christ is Lord of day and night (time), things visible and invisible, and life and death. On the back side of the Cross at this location are the Alpha and Omega, the first and last letters of the Greek alphabet, symbolizing the beginning and the end (eternity).
  • H. Quatrefoils (set of four leaves), symbolize the four Evangelists and the four cardinal virtues: Prudence, Justice, Temperance, and Fortitude (Wisdom 8:7).
  • I. Cross Grade, the three steps, from top down, stand for Faith, Hope, and Love.

The Saint James Altar Cross presently used in the Library Chapel is also a Roman Budded Cross, with the IHS (first three Greek letters in "Jesus"). This Cross and matching candles were designed and made by Samuel B. Stroup, a member of All Saints.

The Christ Window, over the altar

Faceted glass windows are created by embedding thick pieces of glass in a strong epoxy, and covering the epoxy with sand. The great brilliance is achieved by having the colored glass protrude beyond the epoxy surface. Because of the strong refraction utilized in the process, the windows are less than 50% glass.

I. Trinity Panel

A composite of symbols reminding us that God Himself is the Source of all things and of all knowledge.

  • Three Circles. Three: the Trinity (God). Circles: Eternity, unending.
  • Two Triangles. The two triangles are interlocked forming the Star of David, reminding us of God's revelation of Himself to the Hebrews. Also known as the Creator Star.
  • A Cross is emblazoned over the composite of symbols to remind us of God's perfect revelation in Jesus Christ.

II. Christ Panel

The Reigning Christ, the focal point of this window.

  • The Crown and scepter over His head symbolize his Reigning Glory.
  • The extended left hand symbolizes His calling all men unto Himself.
  • The right hand symbolizes His Benediction of Peace for all who come to Him.
  • The word ALLELUIA is Latin meaning "Praise the Lord."

III. Old Testament Panel

Reminds us of our heritage from God's "chosen People of Israel."

  • Staff and Knife, symbolizes Abraham, the Father of the Hebrews. The Staff symbolizes his position as Patriarch; the Knife, his strong faith revealed in the intended offering of his son, Isaac, as a sacrifice to God.
  • The Burning Bush. Under the leadership of Moses, God welded together his scattered and enslaved people. The symbol of the Burning Bush reminds us of Moses' call "to deliver (my people) out of the hands of the Egyptians" (Exodus 3:8), and to lead them into the Promised Land.
  • The Mantel and the Fiery Chariot. Elijah the prophet, who above all others symbolizes the prophetic Voice of God to his people. The Mantel symbolizes the prophetic responsibility, which Elijah laid upon the shoulders of Elisha, his successor. The Fiery Chariot symbolizes the legend of Elijah's being taken up into Heaven.

Moses and Elijah together symbolize the "Law and the Prophets," the most ancient division of the Hebrew Scripture. Moses is the "Law-Giver" and Elijah, "The Prophet." The Hebrew Scripture "Law and the Prophets" was fulfilled in the Person and Work of Jesus Christ.

  • Gloria, Latin, meaning praise, honor, magnificence, etc. It is an exclamation of praise to God's Presence.

IV. The New Testament Panel

Based upon the great "I Am" sayings in St. John's Gospel account.

  • "I am the Bread of Life" (John 6:35, wheat)
  • "I am the Light of the World" (John 8:12, torch)
  • "I am the Door" (John 10:9, door)
  • "I am the Good Shepherd" (John 10:11, staff)
  • "I am the Resurrection and the Life" (John 11:25, torch, gate)
  • "I am the Way, Truth, and Life" (John 14:6, all)
  • "I am the Vine" (John 15:1, intertwined colors in glass)

The "I AM" sayings are climaxed in John 8:31-59, when Jesus tells the Jews: "Truly, truly, I say to you, before Abraham was, I AM" . . . and they attempted to stone him for blasphemy. Jesus was associating His Person and Work with God, Himself. In Exodus 3:3-14 God reveals to Moses that His Name is "I AM who I AM," and directs Moses to tell the Israelites that "I AM has sent me to you."

The word SANCTUS in the lower portion of this panel is the Latin "Holy," denoting purity, perfection, and blinding separateness from the profane.

V. John the Baptist Panel

It was John whom God used to bridge the chasm between the Old Testament revelation (Panel III) and his revelation in our Lord Jesus Christ (Panel II). The ancient symbol of the Lamb and Flag used for Saint John the Baptist ties together God (Panel I), the Old Testament or Old Covenant (Panel III), and God's revelation in Christ, the New Testament or New Covenant (Panel III).

VI. Virgin Mary Panel

Mary was God's willing human instrument for the event of the Incarnation. The symbol used here for the Blessed Virgin Mother is the stylized letter "M"; a Cross centered in and rising out of the "M"; and a Lily symbolizing purity, love, and willingness. This panel ties together God (Panel I), the Son (Panel II), and the work of the Son (Panel IV).

The Saints Window, in the choir loft

I. The Christ Panel

The Greek letters CHI and RHO are the monogram of the first two letters of the Greek word for Christ. CHI = X; RHO = P; XPISTOS. This topmost panel reminds us that the saints are citizens of the Kingdom of which Christ is the Head.

II. The Saint Peter Panel

The figure of Saint Peter stands under the "Keys to the Kingdom" (see Matthew 16:13-23), which symbolizes Peter's confession of faith as the key to our entering the Kingdom of God. Peter holds in his right hand a Cross and in his left hand the scroll naming the saints of God (Revelation 6:9-11).

III. The Saint James the Great Panel

  • Symbols representing the Voice of God breaking through the clouds on the mountain top where Peter, James, and John experienced our Lord's Transfiguration in the presence of Moses and Elijah. (See Matthew 17:1-8; Mark 9:2-8; Luke 9:28-36.)
  • The Seal of Saint James the Great contains the escallop shell, symbol of his pilgrimages by the sea; and the sword of martyrdom, symbolizing that he was the first of the Apostles to give his life for the faith (See Acts 12:2).

Saint James (referred to as "The Great" in contrast to Saint James "The Less," the brother of our Lord) is always listed among the closest inner circle of Christ's apostles. Very little is known about him. He was the brother of Saint John, the Apostle (not the Evangelist, writer of the Fourth Gospel). These two brothers were the sons of Zebedee, a fisherman. Noted for their quick tempers, they were given the nickname "sons of thunder" (Mark 3:17). James is the patron saint of Spain, but it is unlikely that any of the legends connecting him to that country have substance.

  • The Olive Branch, symbolizing St. James' close presence to our Lord in the Garden of Gethsemane on the night before his death.

IV. The All Saints Panel

Three fish fashioned into a triangle for Trinity. The fish is one of the oldest Christian symbols. The letters F-I-S-H in Greek comprise initials for the phrase "Jesus Christ, Son of God, Savior." The fish was a secret greeting sign for Christians during times of persecution. The Trinity form reminds us that God was incarnate in Christ.

The All Saints Seal:

  • The Wings at the top of the seal remind us that the saints in heaven are at one with the saints on earth.
  • The Crown symbolizes the "Crown of Life" for all the saints who follow in the Way of the Master. The crown is often used to symbolize Christ's Reign in Glory.
  • The Cross of Triumph symbolizes the triumph of the Gospel throughout the earth. The Cross is a kingly gold and the earth is green, a symbol of life and growth. The blue insets above the cross symbolize heaven.
  • The Alpha and Omega, first and last letters of the Greek alphabet, on either side of the Cross of Triumph symbolize the eternity of the Kingdom of God.
  • The Dogwood Branch at the bottom of the seal recalls our Lord's saying: "I am the Vine, you are the branches" (John 15:5). We are reminded that Christ is the source of our life both on earth and in heaven.

The Mission Cross, with its arms pointing to the four points of the compass, symbolizes our Lord's command for his people to "go into all the world" with the message of His saving Gospel. (Matthew 28:19)

V. The Saint Stephen Panel

Saint Stephen, Deacon, was the first Christian martyr. Stephen's cloak reminds us that Saul, later called Paul, consented to his death by stoning. (Acts 7:54 – 8:1)

VI. The Saint Paul Panel

St. Paul was the great missionary who first carried the Gospel into Europe. The Seal of Saint Paul reminds us that even among the staunchest of the Pharisees and traditionalists were those who came to faith in God's revelation in Jesus Christ. The open Bible and the Sword symbolize the Word of God as "the Sword of the Spirit" (Gladius Spiritus).

The Sacramental Windows, in the parlor

A Sacrament is "an outward and visible sign of an inward and spiritual grace given unto us; ordained by Christ Himself, as a means whereby we receive this grace, and a pledge to assure us thereof." (Prayer Book, page 292)

There are two Sacraments: Holy Baptism and Holy Communion. In Baptism we are by the grace of God made members of the One, Holy, Catholic, and Apostolic Church. In the Holy Communion (or Eucharist, Greek for "Thanksgiving") we receive in a special way the living Presence of Jesus Christ, and gain strength for our lives.

The Holy Baptism Window (left side)

"Go and Baptize all Nations In The Name of the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit" (The Great Commandment of Matthew 28:19). The Shell dripping Water symbolizes the Sacrament of Baptism.

  • The Hand of God (Manus Dei) pointing downward from a Cloud of Glory symbolizes both the creative Power of God and His openness to all people. The tri-radiant nimbus behind the Hand signifies divinity.
  • The Descending Dove is the earliest symbol for the presence of the Holy Spirit, and is based on the account of our Lord's Baptism. (Matthew 3:16; Mark 1:10; Luke 3:22; John 1:32)
  • The Font reminds of our own Baptism, who baptizes our Lord, and shortly afterwards proclaimed Him "Lamb of God."

The Holy Communion Window (right side)

"This is My Body and This is My Blood. Do this in remembrance of Me." (from the Last Supper)

  • The Chalice and Communion Bread symbolize the Blessed Sacrament of our Lord's Body and Blood. The Cross of Saint Chad, Bishop of Litchfield, England (died 679) reminds us of the great sacrifice that Christ anticipated as he instituted this Supper.
  • The PAX (Latin "Peace") calls us to the "Peace of God which passes all understanding."
  • The Grapes symbolize the Blood (Wine) of our Lord Jesus Christ.
  • The Fleur-de-lis (French "Flower of the Lily") symbolizes the Holy Trinity and reminds us of the Triune Nature of God.
  • The Wheat symbolizes the Body (Bread) of our Lord Jesus Christ.
The Seasonal Windows

I. The Creation Window

"In the beginning was the Word and the Word was God" (Saint John 1:1)

  • The Holy Name "YAHWEH" (Hebrew, "The Lord") is centered in a Circle, symbolizing eternity. The rays emanating from the Circle symbolize the blinding Glory of the Lord God.
  • The three Circles (symbolizing eternity) intertwined with the Triangle symbolize the Godhead as the Three Persons of the Trinity: Father, Son, and Holy Spirit.
  • The ALPHA and OMEGA (left and right sides respectively) are the first and last letters of the Greek alphabet, and symbolize the beginning and the end; that the Lord was before all time and is beyond all time, and that all things are under His Providence.
  • The Fish is one of the oldest Christian symbols. The letters in the Greek word "fish" compose the initials of the acrostic phrase meaning "Jesus Christ, Son of God, Savior." We are reminded that the most perfect revelation of the Lord God is Jesus Christ.

The Scripture in the window reminds us that Jesus Christ is the Incarnation of the Creative Power of God, that "The Word became flesh and dwelt among us" (John 1:14). This Creation Window does not commemorate a Church Season, but rather places our windows in the context of God's eternity.

II. The Advent Window

"Prepare the way of the Lord" (St. Matthew 3:3, etc.) "The Kingdom of God is at hand." (St. Matthew 3:2, etc.)

  • The "TAU" Cross (so called because of resemblance to the Greek letter "tau") is the original form of the Cross. Also called the Cross of Prophecy or Advent Cross. The Sun of Righteousness rises in flaming glory, symbolizing the Advent ("coming") of the Christ ("Messiah").
  • The Flaming Torch symbolizes the burning prophecy of the righteous God, calling men to repent and prepare for the coming of the Messiah.
  • The Anchor Cross symbolizes salvation from sin through the merits of the Savior's death and resurrection, and hope in life eternal, the hope that is promised to us all in Jesus Christ. "Anchored in Faith!"
  • The Shell and Flag symbolize John the Baptist, who prepared the way of the Lord. John is the central Advent character.
  • A variation on the Anchor Cross.

"Behold a virgin shall conceive and bear a son . . ." (Isaiah 7:14)

III. The Christian Window

"And the Word became flesh and dwelt among us" (John 1:14)

  • The Manger in the radiant glory of the Star symbolizes the Incarnation Event.
  • The Christmas Rose symbolizes the Virgin Mary.
  • IHS, the first letters of the Greek name "Jesus," and CHI RHO, the first letters of the Greek name "Christ."
  • The Carpenter's Mallet and Square symbolize Saint Joseph, our Lord's human father. We are further reminded that Jesus was trained in this work.

ANGEL (Hebrew and Greek, "Messenger"): a heavenly being created by God and who stands in His presence ready to do His bidding.

IV. The Epiphany Window

"We have seen His star and have come to worship Him." (Matthew 2:3) "I am the way, the truth, and the life." (John 14:16, and others)

  • The Epiphany (Greek, "manifestation") Star reminds us that through the manifestation of the Star to the wise men the Incarnate Word ("King") was made known to the Gentile peoples.
  • The Egyptian Forms symbolize the Holy Family's flight into Egypt to escape Herod's jealous wrath.
  • The Candle symbolizes that the Light of God shone even through the darkness of men's hearts. (John 1:6-13)
  • The Gold, Frankincense, and Myrrh remind us of the gifts brought by the wise men. Gold represents our gifts of substance, material things. Frankincense, by its fragrance, represents our inner treasure of thought and influence, intangible gifts. Myrrh, by its use in medicine and embalming, represents our sorrow and suffering, the "bitter gift." Because of their great value, these gifts were considered appropriate for a monarch.
  • The Knife symbolizes Herod's murder of the young male children in an effort to rid himself of the King for whom the wise men searched. We are reminded that the world often seeks to do away with God's Word.

V. The Lent Window

"Is it nothing to you and all you who pass by?" (Lamentations 1:12)

  • The Cross and Crown of Thorns symbolize the Passion and Death of our Lord.
  • The Whips symbolize the ridicule and scourging of our Lord.
  • The Palm Branches symbolize our Lord's Triumphant entry into Jerusalem the Sunday before his death.
  • The Cup of Submission and the pointed Cross of Agony symbolize the Last Supper, instituted by our Lord on the night before His Death. This Cross of Agony also symbolizes His agony in the Garden of Gethsemane.
  • The Purse and Thirty Pieces of Silver symbolize our Lord's betrayal and arrest.

The twelve crosses shown (left to right):

  1. Maltese Cross, of Crusade origin, used by the Hospitallers, whose headquarters was in Malta. Known also as the Regeneration Cross, symbolizing new birth (expanding from the center), with the eight outer points symbolizing the Beatitudes.
  2. Latin (Roman) Cross, very old and the most used Cross symbol.
  3. Saint Andrew's Cross. Andrew requested to be crucified on a cross unlike that of his Lord.
  4. Tau Cross, the original form of the cross. Used for crucifixions.
  5. Cross Crosslet, represents Christianity spreading in four directions. Often used as Mission Cross.
  6. Celtic Cross (or Cross of Iona). The circle (eternity) symbolizes Christ's sacrifice for all people of all times.
  7. Patriarchal Cross. The upper (shorter) cross arm represents the inscription that was placed over our Lord's head.
  8. Botonnee (Budded) Cross. The trefoil ends remind us that God (the Three In One) made His perfect Sacrifice in Jesus Christ.
  9. Anchor Cross, symbolizes salvation by our Lord's Death and Resurrection, and hope in life eternal. ("Anchored in the Faith")
  10. Greek Cross, an old variation of the Cross.
  11. Passion ("Agony") Cross. The pointed ends remind us of the sharpness of our Lord's agony.
  12. Crux Ansata, from the key-like Egyptian "ankh," a symbol of life and immortality. Christians appropriated this as a symbol.

The Scripture in the window, first written to confess sorrow over the destruction of Jerusalem by the Babylonians (586 B.C.), reminds us to consider the tragedy of man's sin that caused the great sacrifice. The Plumb Line: God's Righteous Judgment and "Behold, I am setting a plumb line in the midst of my people." (Amos 7:8)

VI. The Easter Window

"Alleluia! The Lord is risen indeed!" (Ancient Easter greeting) "My Lord and My God" (John 20:28)

  • The Risen and Triumphant Lamb of God (Agnus Dei) stands victorious before the Cross of death. The rays of glorious light reflect off the Circle, reminding us that His victory is forever.
  • The Lily symbolizes the Resurrection by reminding us that a bulb dying in the soil produces a new bulb, stem, leaves, and flowers, all rising in glory above the darkness of the soil; signifying the attainment of immortal life though the body perishes.
  • The Phoenix, the mythical bird which at death bursts into flames but rises from its own ashes, symbolizes Christ's resurrection, and our immortal life in Him.
  • The Bursting Pomegranate, split by the pressure of its many seeds, symbolizes the power to reproduce life. It also symbolizes the new life for all people in Christ's bursting forth from death.
  • The Butterfly, leaving its "tomb" in the pupa, soars upward symbolizing Christ's Resurrection, and that through Christ His followers are borne to a new life.

Jesus Christ the Victor: the Greek Cross, the first and last letters of the Greek words for Jesus Christ, and the Greek word NIKA, meaning "victor."

VII. The Ascension Window

"Christ the Lord has Ascended Into Heaven" (Ancient Ascension greeting) "And He shall reign forever and ever!" (Revelation 11:15)

  • The Fiery Chariot is an ancient symbol of our Lord's Ascension; taken from the Old Testament, "Behold there appeared a chariot of fire . . . and Elijah went up by a whirlwind into Heaven." (II Kings 2:11)
  • The Christus Victor: the Cross with a RHO at the top symbolizes the Greek letters CHI-RHO, the first letters in the name Jesus Christ; and the Greek letter N for NIKA, which means "victor." Thus, "Christ the Victor" (Christus Victor).
  • The Cross of Triumph symbolizes the reign of Christ over all the world.
  • The Crown and Scepter are symbols of Christ's reign in heaven and on earth.

VIII. The Pentecost Window

"They were with one accord in one place." "And they were filled with the Holy Spirit." (Acts 2:1-4)

  • The Descending Dove comes directly from the Baptism of our Lord, when "the heavens opened and the Spirit descended upon Him like a dove" (Matthew 3:13-17; Mark 1:9-11; Luke 3:21-22). This same Spirit, the Third Person of the Trinity, came upon the Disciples at Pentecost (Acts 2:1-4), and comes upon us at our Baptism when we are made members of Christ's Church. The Nimbus with three rays circling the head of the Dove signifies divinity.
  • The Seven Flames remind us that when the Spirit came upon the Disciples at Pentecost "there appeared to them tongues as of fire . . . resting on each of them" (Acts 2:3). The number seven symbolizes the seven gifts of the Spirit: Power, Riches, Wisdom, Strength, Honor, Glory, and Blessing (Revelation 5:12). The gifts of the Spirit referred to in the Confirmation service (Prayer Book, Page 297) are: Grace, Wisdom, Understanding, Council, Ghostly strength, Knowledge, True Godliness (Isaiah 11:2).
  • The Ten Commandments on Tables of Stone remind us of our heritage from the Old Testament. Pentecost means "fiftieth" day, and was the Jewish Feast of Weeks (Thanksgiving for the grain harvest). It was also a commemoration of the giving of the Law on Mount Sinai, and thus the foundation of "the Jewish Church."
  • The Tower of Babel reminds us that when men tried to build a tower into heaven (Genesis 11:1-9), that is to "storm God's throne," they were scattered across the face of the earth and their language was confused. At the Christian Pentecost the Power of the Holy Spirit inspired the Apostles to speak in a manner that all men understood (Acts 2:5-11). The name Babel = Babylon ("gate of God"). The Hebrews used this word to mean "to confuse," "to mix."
  • The Ship is an ancient symbol for the Church, which came into being at Pentecost. The Ship sails unharmed through all perils. The word "Nave" comes from the Latin word for "ship."
  • The Vine symbolizes Christ as the source of life for the "Branches," the Church. "I am the Vine, you are the Branches" (John 15:5)

The English and other northern European peoples called the Day of Pentecost WHITSUNDAY ("White Sunday") from the white garments worn by the newly baptized on this day. "The Feast of the Coming of The Holy Spirit" has long been an appropriate day for Baptism.
